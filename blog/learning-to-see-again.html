Learning to see, again

September 5, 2026

In 2019 I sold all of my camera gear, a mirrorless camera and three lenses, a little point-and-shoot I carried alongside it, and a GoPro. The whole kit had been sitting in a drawer for almost a year by then because I had broken into digital design the year before and there was so much to learn and not enough hours for it, so photography took a backseat and the backseat became a drawer. It was a deliberate choice, and I didn’t feel like I was losing anything so much as making room for the thing I wanted more.

In June 2024 I wrote in my journal: “Sometimes I wonder if growing up has made me lose something, something magical in the way I used to experience everything. I used to have a hobby that made me feel so alive.” That August I was watching photography videos on YouTube again, missing the days when taking photos was the thing that nourished me, and looking at what a full frame camera would cost, because I’ve only had APS-C ones, and if I was going to do this “properly” I figured I’d go for it finally, at which point the whole shopping list appeared: lenses, memory cards, extra batteries, accessories, then a Lightroom subscription on top of everything. I knew photography would bring me joy but I said no anyway, deciding I was probably just being nostalgic. It wasn’t the camera that killed the idea but rather everything the camera would need around it.

For a while my phone was honestly enough. The iPhone 11 Pro was my trusty camera for years, the 15 Pro with Zerocam got me through Europe and Taiwan, and when the iPhone 17 came out I went with the base model, mostly for the newer image processing, which to my eye makes photos look far more natural, thinking I didn’t really use RAW or the zoom anyway. It turned out I did miss the zoom, since the 2x on the 17 is digital rather than optical and never quite enough, I’ve never liked the wide lens, and no camera app I tried gave back the tactile feeling I didn’t know I was looking for. The other thing that crept up slowly was that all my travel photos from all these big trips looked like iPhone photos, because they were, and the distinct iPhone look I used to think was on purpose had become something I wasn’t proud of. I had been thinking about a photos page on my site before I ever thought about buying a camera again, and every time I scrolled through years of trips nothing felt like it really deserved a gallery.

In early August this year I went back and forth on it in my journal again and still decided I wouldn’t do it. A week later I was at 50/50, mostly because I was stuck on where the photos would even go. But then just a week after, I put down a deposit on an RX100 VII — funnily enough the same line as the compact I sold in 2019, the RX100 V, so I guess I’m back where I left off. Not the a7C II I wanted in 2024, not a bag of lenses, not the Ricoh GR IV or Fujifilm X100, which are bigger, pricier (here in Vietnam, at least), and fixed at one focal length. After a week of YouTube reviews it came down to one thing: size. The RX100 is small enough to disappear into a pocket, which means I can be discreet with it, and it zooms to 200mm! The rest somehow fell into place around that: my fiancée found a deal directly with Sony Vietnam which came with an extra battery pack, old SD cards from 2018 turned up in a storage box, and an afternoon trying Photomator convinced me I don’t need Lightroom. That was the whole system, and it’s low maintenance. This time it’s not about the gear, or at least that’s what I’m trying to tell myself. Either way, I bring it out, find an angle, snap a shot, and it goes back in my pocket. First outing was already booked: ten days in Chongqing, Chengdu, and Jiuzhaigou, our second trip to China in six months and the first with something better than a phone.

I started watching tutorials a few days before the camera arrived, mostly on framing and composition and editing, and continued on hotel nights during the trip while I looked for ideas for the next day’s shots — basically homework for a camera on the way. And boy was I rusty. Seems I had forgotten all about the exposure compensation dial, metering modes, and ISO range, and my first few photos after I got the camera had no depth and were basically not that much better than what came out of my phone. After years of letting my phone decide white balance, aperture, and shutter speed for me, I had become quite passive. It’s just so effortless to point it at something then tap a button. It took me a day or two to get the basics back.

I notice what changed most is the few seconds or minutes before the shot. I used to snap things because they looked interesting at a glance, and now I try to ask what I want the photo to say, and to be patient with myself while I figure that out. At Huangjuewan Park in Chongqing you look out over the interchange everyone calls the spaghetti junction, five levels of ramps stacked on top of each other and heading off in eight directions. Usually I would have pointed the phone at the whole tangle and moved on after a couple of wide angle shots. Instead I picked one ramp curving out of the trees, zoomed in, and waited for the traffic to thin until there was exactly one car on it. It took a few minutes, I like how it turned out.

My fiancée is into history, museums, and anything that built the place we’re standing in, and I mostly just want to chill, eat, and look at everything in the present tense. Before the camera I was bored to death at temples and museums — not immediately, but by the second one of the day I was done. On this trip she went to temples and I tagged along with my camera, so while she read the plaques I looked for angles, learning to see again. At Wenshu Monastery in Chengdu I found one: a red hall with calligraphy running down the pillars, potted trees along the base, a branch hanging into the top of the frame, and a steady stream of people walking through it. I wanted it empty. I stood there a while waiting for the courtyard to clear, and when it finally did, the one person left in it was her. I took the shot before I’d decided to, and I think it turned out better than the empty one would have been.

Back in my vlogging days the camera intruded on the moment because I put the moment aside to record it, with a rig in front of my face, and the enjoying happened later at home. This one sits in the palm of my hand with the strap around my wrist, or in my pocket when I don’t feel like holding it, and either way it’s barely noticeable. It doesn’t feel like I’m there to take photos. It feels like I’m there, and I get to keep some of it for later. And sometimes taking photos is the experience, which I’d forgotten how much I enjoyed.

After ten days, I’m ready to go home and get back to my routine. There are close to 600 photos on the SD card, I’ve only had time to edit three of them, and there’s at least a dozen I really like. The process has been light: clean up whatever distracts from the subject, tweak the white balance, play around with the colors a little. As for where they’ll go, the answer is the photos page on this site I’d been thinking about before the camera, a project on its own. I considered Flickr, which I loved back in the day, but its UI feels so old now. My old gallery is still there, alas locked behind an email I no longer have access to. My old Instagram is still around too.

I’ll miss the phone surfacing old trips at random, which is the one perk of having every photo from the last few years in the Photos app. And let’s see how long before I talk myself into the full frame.
